Compliance home, four ways

Four answers to one brief: an office screen for a business that keeps other people's buildings legal. Same materials as the approved entry screens and the Developments drafts. What changes between them is which question the page answers first — what is due, where do we stand, which building is exposed, or what is this asset.

Kept
The hub's charcoal, grey and off-white. Archivo and Plex Mono. Hairline rules, strict grid, no cards.
Accent roles — proposed
Aquamarine marks the company, references and active state, the job brass does in Developments. Gold stays on the one primary action per screen, as on the hub.
Status language
Four states everywhere: in date, due (inside 30 days), overdue, no record. A glyph and a word carry each; colour only weights them.
Made up throughout
Clients, buildings, assets, dates, engineers and report types are all invented in a plausible Leeds format. Nothing here is wired to the application.
Clients
Who we answer toPortfolio owners and managing agents. 5 in the examples.
Buildings
Where the duty sitsEach has a responsible person and a set of statutory requirements. 14 in the examples.
Assets
What gets servicedFire alarm, emergency lighting, boilers, lifts, doors, water systems. 412 in the examples.
Details
The dates that matterLast service, next due, warranty end, certificates, remedials, and the reports raised against it.
How the brief was read
  • A compliance office runs on dates against a regime, not on visits. So the primary object on every screen is a due date with the requirement that sets it, and the status vocabulary is the same on all four.
  • The hierarchy you gave — Clients › Buildings › Assets › Details — is drawn literally in Concept 2 and used as the breadcrumb on every asset elsewhere. It is the navigation structure whichever home wins.
  • Warranties are tracked separately from service. A boiler can be in date for its gas safety check while its warranty runs out next month; the two dates never share a column.
  • Reports are shown as options against an asset — service report, test certificate, remedial works report, condition survey, building compliance statement — with none of them claimed to exist yet.
Concept 1

Due desk

Time-first. The office's day is a list of things that fall due, so the page is that list, sorted by date, with the overdue at the top and everything the manager can act on within reach. A permanent rail carries the register — clients, buildings, assets — so the hierarchy is always one click away without being the page.

What it gets right
  • The list is the job. A compliance manager opens the day asking what is due; this answers it with no navigation at all.
  • Overdue and due are visibly different things, and “no record” is its own state, so a blank never passes for compliant.
  • The attention band says who is exposed — the duty holder — not just what number is late.
What it assumes
  • A requirements table: each asset category mapped to its standard and interval, so due dates compute from the last service.
  • Warranty end stored per asset, separate from the service record.
  • A remedial entity linked to the report that raised it.
Trade-off
  • Flat. Two overdue items in the same building appear as two rows, and the office has to notice they are one visit.
  • A client asking “how are we doing?” gets no answer here; that lives in the register.
Concept 2

Portfolio browser

The hierarchy, drawn literally. Four columns, left to right: clients, the buildings of the selected client, the assets in the selected building, and the selected asset's details. Every column shows its compliance position, so a manager can see which client is exposed, then which building, then which asset, without the page ever changing. It is a file browser for a portfolio.

What it gets right
  • It is the hierarchy. Clients › Buildings › Assets › Details is the screen, not a breadcrumb. Nobody has to learn the structure.
  • The compliance bar answers “how are we doing?” at every level in the same shape, so a client conversation and a building visit read the same way.
  • Selecting an asset never leaves the page; the details column is the asset page.
What it assumes
  • Roll-ups at client and building level: counts of in date, due, overdue, no record.
  • A stable asset categorisation, since the third column groups by it.
  • Selection state across four columns, which is a proper piece of front-end work.
Trade-off
  • “What is due this week across everyone?” is not on this screen — it is behind the Due tab.
  • Needs width. Below about 1300px the fourth column has to become a drawer.
  • A client with 40 buildings turns column two into a scroll.

Every cell is a status and a date; select one and the assets behind it appear below. The week strip under the matrix shows how the next two months load up.

What it gets right
  • One glance for the whole portfolio. The exposed cells find your eye; everything green stays quiet.
  • It is the document the client already understands — this replaces a spreadsheet they have seen a hundred times.
  • The week strip turns compliance into a staffing question, which is what the office actually has to solve.
What it assumes
  • A fixed requirement taxonomy as columns — the ten here are a guess at Leodis's actual scope.
  • Per-cell roll-up when several assets sit behind one requirement (39 fire doors in one cell).
  • An “applies / does not apply” flag per building and requirement.
Trade-off
  • Wide. Ten columns is the limit before it scrolls; a wider scope means grouping columns.
  • A cell hides its detail; two overdue assets and one look identical until selected.
  • Looks like a scorecard, and clients may read it as one. The wording has to stay factual.

Asset-first. This is the screen where the office does the actual work of keeping records straight, and where the report options you mentioned would live.

What it gets right
  • The record is the page. Every date, document and report about an asset in one place, which is what an auditor or a client's insurer asks for.
  • Warranty sits beside the service date, so the “bring it forward” judgement is visible instead of remembered.
  • Report types are listed against the asset, honestly marked by what exists.
What it assumes
  • An asset record with make, model, serial, location, install date and installer.
  • Certificates as documents attached to a service event, not free files.
  • “Same visit” grouping by location, a small but valuable rule.
Trade-off
  • Poor as a home: it answers “what is this?” brilliantly and “how are we doing?” not at all.
  • A 460px list carries less per row than Concept 1's table; the client name is a subtitle.
  • This is the asset page whichever concept wins; as a home it is a detail view promoted above its station.

Choosing between them

ConceptAnswers firstBest whenBuild cost from today
1 · Due desk “What is due, and what is late?” The office runs a daily list and books visits from it. Medium. Needs the requirements table and a due-date computation; the register table is familiar.
2 · Portfolio browser “Where do we stand, client by client?” Managers think in clients and buildings, and want the hierarchy to be the navigation. Medium–high. Roll-ups at two levels plus a four-column selection model.
3 · Compliance matrix “Which building is exposed on which requirement?” Client reviews and audits; replacing the spreadsheet. High. A fixed taxonomy, applies/does-not-apply flags and per-cell roll-ups.
4 · Asset ledger and reader “What is this asset and what has happened to it?” Record-keeping, audits, raising reports. Low–medium as an asset page. As a home it is the wrong altitude.

My recommendation: Concept 1 as the home, Concept 2 as the structure, Concept 4 as the asset page

These are less rivals than layers. The home should be the due desk. A compliance business is a calendar with consequences, and the screen the office opens each morning should be the list of what falls due, loudest at the top. Concept 1 does that with the least new machinery and the most direct payoff.

The navigation should be Concept 2. Clients › Buildings › Assets is the structure you gave, and the compliance bar at each level is how “how are we doing?” gets answered on the phone. It does not need to be the landing page to be the spine of the product; it is where the Register links in the rail go.

The asset page should be Concept 4's reader, with the report options listed against the asset and marked by what exists. The record is what everything else summarises, so it should be the richest screen, not the first one.

Hold the matrix until the requirement taxonomy is settled. It is the most impressive of the four and the most dependent on decisions nobody has made yet — which requirements Leodis covers, and how a building declares that one does not apply. Built early, it would be a scorecard full of “no record”.

Two things to settle before any of it: the accent roles (aquamarine structural, gold for the action — proposed here, not approved), and the status vocabulary. In date, due, overdue, no record — four words, used identically on every screen, is most of what makes a compliance tool trustworthy.

All four are static mockups. Nothing is wired to the application, and the Compliance route in the app remains an honest “not yet available” page. Clients, buildings, assets, standards intervals, engineers, dates and report types are examples in a plausible format, chosen to show how the screens behave; the statutory intervals quoted are typical UK practice and would need confirming against Leodis's actual scope before they drive anything.
