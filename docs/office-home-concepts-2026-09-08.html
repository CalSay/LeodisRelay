Office home, three ways

Three different answers to the same brief: treat the office screen as a website rather than a phone page stretched wide. Same palette, same typefaces, same register idiom — what changes is the information architecture underneath.

Kept
Charcoal, brass and paper exactly as they are. Archivo and Plex Mono. Hairline rules, no floating cards.
Adjusted for separation
A recessed ground behind list regions, so panels read as surfaces. Region edges move to the heavier rule.
Fixed throughout
Delivery failures carry their severity. “Obs” becomes “updates”. The empty review queue is gone.
Assumed, not built
Issues on the office screen, search, filters, a visit planner. Marked per concept below.
Second pass — what changed
  • The four-figure tile strip is gone from Concept 1. It was the generic dashboard move, one figure was invented, and it pushed the register down the page.
  • The register now says what each visit found — defects, access problems, variations — instead of counting updates. Engineer gets its own column.
  • Concept 2’s reader now follows the real document: action and decision summary first, then the updates, then the signature line.
  • Attention items have actions, not links. Counts agree with each other.
  • Concept 4 added — the combination I described in words last time, now drawn: rail and attention band from 1, the reader from 2, and 3’s project-first view as a strip of filter tiles rather than a board.
Concept 1

Control desk

A permanent left rail carries the whole product, so the office is never more than one click from anything. The screen opens with what is wrong, then the register, then a rail of standing work. This is the most conventional of the three, and the most extensible — every future section is a rail item.

What it fixes
  • Failures are loud. The attention band is the only place on the screen allowed to use alert colour, so it means something.
  • No empty review queue. The section appears only when a project requires review.
  • Full width earns its keep — register and standing work side by side.
What it assumes
  • An issues list on the office screen — the biggest missing piece today.

Trade-off

  • Reading a report still means leaving the page.
Concept 2

Register and reader

The office spends its day reading reports, so the report is on the page. A dense list on the left, the selected document on the right, the way a mail client works. Nobody navigates away to find out what an engineer actually said, and checking ten reports is ten clicks in one place.

Concept 3

Project board

Organised the way the office already thinks. Nobody asks “what reports came in?” — they ask “where are we on Tetley Hall?”. Each live project is one panel carrying its last visit, its open issues and its delivery state, with a visit planner underneath showing which sites have gone quiet.

What it fixes
  • Matches how the office asks the question. Project first, documents second.
  • Issues and delivery sit against the project they belong to, so nothing is orphaned.
  • The planner surfaces silence — the one thing a report list can never show.
What it assumes

  • A link straight into the SharePoint project folder.

Trade-off
  • Scales badly past roughly a dozen live projects without a filter or collapse.
  • “What came in today?” takes one more click than it does in Concept 1.
  • The planner needs care: coverage is one sentence away from reading as a productivity score.
Concept 4

Combined desk

One screen that answers all three questions in order: what is wrong, where are we on each job, and what does this report actually say. The rail and attention band come from Concept 1. The project strip is Concept 3 reduced to a row of tiles that filter everything beneath them. The reader is Concept 2, sharing the screen with the register instead of replacing it, and it carries the open issues on the same project as the report being read — the context a manager reaches for next.

What it takes from each
  • 1 — the rail and the attention band, unchanged. Structure that lasts and one loud place.
  • 2 — the reader, condensed to a column. The document is on the page; the round trip is gone.
  • 3 — project-first, as filter tiles. Open work and days since a visit on every tile; “gone quiet” becomes an attention item rather than a planner grid.
What it assumes
  • Everything Concept 1 assumed, plus a selected-row state for the reader.
  • Per-project open counts and last-visit dates — cheap: both are one query on data the store already holds.
  • A quiet-site threshold, which is a policy decision, not a technical one.
Trade-off
  • The densest screen of the four. It needs a wide monitor and earns it; on a laptop the reader would need to collapse.
  • The strip stops working past about a dozen tiles — hence “8 more”, which becomes a search.
  • Photos in a 400px reader are thumbnails. Anyone checking evidence opens the PDF.

Choosing between them

ConceptAnswers firstBest whenBuild cost from today
1 · Control desk “What is wrong, and what came in?” The office is triaging across many projects and wants one place to stand. Medium. Needs an issues list and filters; the register is largely what you have.
2 · Register and reader “What does this report actually say?” Most office time goes on reading and checking documents. Low–medium. The reader is the preview page you already render; read state is the new part.
3 · Project board “Where are we on each job?” Reporting to clients and running project meetings from the app. High. Needs roll-ups and visit history that nothing computes yet.
4 · Combined desk All three, in that order. The office wants one screen to stand on for the day, on a proper monitor. Medium–high. Concept 1 plus a selected-row reader and two per-project figures.

My recommendation: Concept 4, built in the order 1 → 2 → 3

Concept 4 is what I described in words last time, drawn out. It is the right destination. It is not the right first build, because it is three things and each of them can ship on its own.

Ship Concept 1 first — the rail, the attention band, the register with a Found column. That fixes the two real defects on today’s screen (a failure styled like a timestamp, and a permanently empty review queue) and gives every later section a place to live.

Then add the reader as a selected-row column, exactly as Concept 4 shows it. The document is already rendered by the preview page; this is mostly layout.

Then the project strip, once the confirmation question is settled and per-project counts mean something. Until then a tile that says “5 open” is counting issues nobody has looked at, and the strip would be teaching the office to ignore it.

Concept 3 as a full board I would still hold — it is the same information as the strip at four times the screen and the build, and the strip is what makes 4 work.

All three are static mockups — nothing here is wired to the application. Data shown is representative, not real: project names, references and engineers are examples in the Leodis format.
